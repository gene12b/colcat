The Central Nervous System (CNS) is an organ dedicated to collecting information from sensory
systems, processing information, persistent storage and recollection of information, pattern 
recognition, and controlling bodily functions to control internal biological
systems and manipulate the environment external to the organism. It is mostly composed of a few
types of cells, primarily neurons, astrocytes, microglia, oligodendrocytes, pericytes, and
ependymyocytes. Neurons play the most important role in the CNS by converting sensory information
into short-lived quantal fluctuations in electrochemical potential called action potentials and 
more permanent changes to inter-neural signaling connections called synapses. Electrical synapses
are gap junctions between neurons, allowing ion flow directly between their cytoplasm. Chemical synapse 
are primarily mono-directional podia-shaped processes extending from neuronal plasma membranes, where one
is transmitting through release of chemicals and another is receiving through signal transduction
by receptors that are activated by coming to contact with the aforementioned released chemical. 
The other cell types are all forms of glial cells, originally referring to the metaphorical glue which holds together
the neurons in the brain cite:[kettenmann_concept_2025]. Astrocytes are the most prevalent glial subtype cite:[freeman_evolving_2013], 
named after their morphological similarities to the shape of a star cite:[verkhratsky_physiology_2018]. Classically they are thought of a 
cell type that existed solely to support neuron health, however they have to be known as 
cells which can directly influence and support neuronal activity in addition to their role in
maintaining neurophysological homeostasis. Chemical synapses typically have a third 
process in addition to the transmitting pre-synaptic and the receiving post-synaptic 
neuronal end belonging to astrocytes. Astrocytes also send out processes that form a water-tight
barrier preventing exposure of the CNS to peripheral elements circulating through the body in
vasculature. Governing these astrocytic physiological processes is calcium signaling. Much like the transistor,
calcium is separated from cytosol by both the plasma membrane and the endoplasmic reticulum membrane,
and respectively act as gates for two different kinds of calcium signaling modalities. Calcium
derived from the exterior is in near-infinite supply, whereas calcium deriving from endoplasmic
reticulum is limited and requires refilling through a process called store operated calcium
entry (SOCE). Disrupting calcium signaling in astrocytes results in changes in the aforementioned
physiological processed they are tasked with. The impact can be seen in changes to neuronal 
activity, tying astrocyte calcium directly to neural network function. This effect is pronounced
in how astrocytes react to the innate and ubiquitous response to abnormal shifts in physiological
environment called inflammation. And, although the CNS is immune
privileged, situations arise in which peripheral pathogens and blunt trauma or deprivation
of oxygen enter the CNS to generate an inflammatory response. Astrocytes, like all cells 
in the body, display marked changes to their behavior in response to pathogens and 
physical injury. Lipopolysaccharide (LPS) is a molecule that mimicks the unique membrane property
of bacteria to have lipid molecules containing sugar carbohydrate elements. Its presence in
tissues reliably generates an inflammatory response, of which the CNS is no exception to. 
LPS-induction in the CNS leads to measurable changes in astrocyte calcium signaling,
and likewise changes to astrocyte calcium signaling results in changes to the astrocytic response
to inflammation. Not only in calcium signaling done conferred by endoplasmic reticulum stores,
but also in maintaining the calcium within those stores. These observations however are primarily
in murine models, and astrocyte physiology is known to differ signficantly between species and
the impact of calcium in one specie's astrocytic process may differ from another's. Induced 
pluripotent stem cell (iPSC) models mimicking embryonic stem cells (ESCs) have been employed to 
examine more clinically relevant human physiology through artificial maturation named
differentiation. A preponderance of cell types have been derived from iPSCs, ranging from accurate
models to whole organisms through tetraploid complementation capable of reproduction. One such
protocol exists for producing cells bearing strong similarity with human primary fetal astrocytes
and is the human model employed in this study to examine the role of inflammation and calcium store
homeostasis. First, the iPSC-derived human astrocytes were examined for biomarkers and 
their ability to perform SOCE was characterized. And then their transcriptomes were 
characterized under LPS-induction and then in combination with SOCE-inhibition to reveal
changes in expression of subsets of signaling pathways involved in inflammation, depression, 
maintainance of neural network structure, and regulated cell death.
